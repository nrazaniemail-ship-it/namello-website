How to Build a Trading Journal That Improves Your Decisions

A useful trading journal does more than store entry and exit prices. It preserves the reasoning, risk and execution behind each decision so you can test whether your process is working.

Updated 2026-11-15 · Educational guide · Not financial advice

Start with the decision, not just the result

A winning trade can come from a poor decision, and a losing trade can follow a well-executed plan. If a journal records only profit and loss, it cannot distinguish process quality from outcome. Capture what you knew when you entered, what would invalidate the idea, and how you managed the position.

The essential fields for each trade

  • Identity: date and time, instrument, direction, market session and strategy or setup label.
  • Trade plan: entry rationale, entry price, stop-loss, target, invalidation level and planned risk.
  • Position: size, account or portfolio context, and the instrument's relevant contract specifications.
  • Execution: actual entry and exit, commissions, spread or other costs where available, and any deviation from the plan.
  • Outcome: net result, result in R, maximum adverse excursion (MAE), maximum favourable excursion (MFE) when reliable price data is available.
  • Review: chart or screenshot, emotional state, rule adherence and one specific lesson.
Keep planned and actual values separate.

Record the intended stop and target before entry, then compare them with what happened. Overwriting the plan with the final execution removes evidence that could help explain the result.

Use a consistent post-trade review

  1. Was the setup valid according to written rules?
  2. Was the initial risk within the predefined limit?
  3. Did execution match the plan, including stop and exit decisions?
  4. Were costs, slippage or market conditions material?
  5. What single behaviour should be repeated or changed?

Review groups, not isolated anecdotes

After collecting a meaningful sample, group trades by strategy, instrument, session, direction or setup. Compare like with like. A strategy may behave differently during high-volatility periods, and a strong aggregate result can hide a weak subgroup. Avoid changing rules after every small run of wins or losses.

Measure the quality of the sample

Check whether records are complete, whether costs are included consistently, and whether the sample covers different market conditions. Separate live, demo and backtest records. Do not combine them without clearly labeling the source and assumptions.

Turn observations into controlled experiments

Choose one hypothesis at a time—for example, whether a time filter improves a particular setup. Define the metric and evaluation period before testing. Compare the result with a baseline, account for costs, and validate changes on unseen data or in a controlled forward test. This reduces the risk of mistaking random variation for a durable edge.

A practical weekly journal routine

  • After each trade: complete the record while the context is still clear.
  • Once a week: review rule adherence, costs and recurring execution errors.
  • Once a month: compare strategy groups and assess whether the sample is large and consistent enough to support a change.

The goal is not to make every trade profitable. It is to make decisions observable, comparable and easier to improve.
