Trading Performance Metrics: Expectancy, Profit Factor, Drawdown and R

No single statistic can describe a trading strategy. A useful performance review combines return, risk, consistency, execution and the quality of the underlying sample.

Updated 2026-11-15 · Educational guide · Not financial advice

1. Win rate: how often trades win

Win rate is the number of profitable trades divided by the total number of trades. It describes frequency, not profitability. A system can win often and still lose money if its average loss is much larger than its average win.

Win rate = Winning trades ÷ Total trades × 100%

2. Average win, average loss and expectancy

Expectancy estimates the average result per trade over a sample. Express all components in the same unit—currency, percentage or R—and use net results consistently.

Expectancy = (Win rate × Average win) − (Loss rate × Average loss)

For example, a 45% win rate with an average win of 2R and an average loss of 1R gives an estimated expectancy of +0.35R per trade before any costs not already included. This is a sample estimate, not a promise about future trades.

3. Profit factor: gross wins relative to gross losses

Profit factor = Gross profit ÷ Absolute gross loss

A value above 1 means gross profits exceeded gross losses in the measured sample. It does not show the size of drawdowns, the number of trades, or whether a few outliers dominate the result. Include commissions and other costs consistently when assessing net performance.

4. Drawdown: the path matters

Drawdown measures a decline from a previous peak to a later trough. Maximum drawdown is the largest such decline over the selected period. State whether it is calculated from balance or equity, and whether it is measured in currency or percentage. Recovery from a loss is asymmetric: a 20% decline requires a 25% gain on the reduced capital just to return to the starting level.

5. R-multiple: compare outcomes against initial risk

R-multiple = Net trade result ÷ Initial monetary risk

R-multiples make trades with different position sizes easier to compare. They are meaningful only when initial risk is defined consistently. If the initial stop or risk amount is missing or changed retrospectively, the calculated R may be misleading.

6. MFE and MAE: inspect trade movement

Maximum favourable excursion (MFE) describes the greatest unrealized move in your favour during a trade. Maximum adverse excursion (MAE) describes the greatest unrealized move against you. They can help review stop placement and exits, but accurate values require sufficiently granular historical price data, correct timestamps and instrument specifications. A statement alone may not contain enough information.

7. Sample size and distribution

Always show the number of trades and the date range beside a metric. Inspect median outcomes, losing streaks, outliers and concentration of profit. Break results down by strategy, symbol, session and market condition only when group sizes are large enough to be informative. Small samples can produce unstable estimates.

8. Build a balanced scorecard

A practical review can include:

  • Net return and average result per trade
  • Win rate, average win and average loss
  • Expectancy and profit factor
  • Maximum drawdown and recovery time
  • R-multiple distribution
  • Trading costs and execution quality
  • Rule adherence and completeness of journal records

Read these measures together. A higher return may come with a larger drawdown; a higher win rate may come with rare but severe losses. The right interpretation depends on objectives, risk limits and the reliability of the data.
